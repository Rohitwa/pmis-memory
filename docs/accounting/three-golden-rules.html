The Three Golden Rules

Every account you will ever open is one of three kinds — personal, real, or nominal. Decide which, and the rule for what to debit and what to credit is already written for you.

Start here

An account is just a page with a name on it

Before any rule makes sense, you need to know what the rules are being applied to.

Imagine a notebook. You give one page to cash, one page to your furniture, one page to your friend Rahul who owes you money, one page to the salaries you pay. Each of those pages is an account. That's the whole idea — an account is a running record of one specific thing.

Now the bit that makes bookkeeping different from a shopping list: every page is split down the middle. The left half is called the debit side. The right half is called the credit side. Because of its shape, bookkeepers call this a T-account.

Unlearn this first

Debit and credit mean left and right. Nothing more.

This is where most beginners quietly get stuck, so let's kill it early. Debit does not mean loss. Credit does not mean gain. They are not good and bad, plus and minus, in and out. They are directions on the page:

Any account whatsoever
DEBIT · Dr
The left side.

From Latin debere, "to owe". In your books it is a position, not a verdict.

CREDIT · Cr
The right side.

From Latin credere, "to entrust". Again — a position, not a verdict.

The second thing to know is the law that governs all of it: every transaction touches at least two accounts, and the amount you put on the left must equal the amount you put on the right. That is what "double entry" means. Money never appears; it only moves from one page to another.

The whole system in one picture

Three questions, asked in order

Take any account name. Walk down this ladder and stop at the first "yes". That is its type, and its type hands you the rule.

The shortcut

Personal answers who. Real answers what. Nominal answers why. If you can shake its hand it's personal; if you can lock it in a cupboard it's real; if it only explains where the money went, it's nominal.

The rules themselves

Three families, three rules

Each family gets exactly one rule, and each rule is a matched pair — one thing to debit, one thing to credit.

Personal accounts

a who

Any account that stands for a human being, a business, a bank, or an institution — anybody who can hand you something or receive something from you. Rahul, HDFC Bank, Infosys Ltd, the local charity, and, importantly, the owner of the business (that's the Capital and Drawings accounts).

Rule for personal accounts
DEBIT
the receiver

Whoever got the benefit goes on the left.

CREDIT
the giver

Whoever handed it over goes on the right.

Feel it: you pay Rahul ₹5,000 in cash. Rahul received — so Dr Rahul A/c ₹5,000. Think of it as writing on Rahul's page: "he took this from me."

Real accounts

a what

Anything the business owns — property and possessions. Cash, furniture, machinery, buildings, land, stock in the godown. Also the things you own that you cannot physically touch: goodwill, patents, trademarks, copyrights. They're called "real" because they stay real from one year to the next — your building doesn't reset on 1 April.

Rule for real accounts
DEBIT
what comes in

The thing arriving into the business goes on the left.

CREDIT
what goes out

The thing leaving the business goes on the right.

Feel it: you buy a table for ₹8,000 cash. Two real accounts are in play. The table came in — Dr Furniture A/c. The cash went out — Cr Cash A/c. One transaction, both sides real, still balances.

Nominal accounts

a why

Accounts that exist only to explain your performance this period: everything you spent or lost, and everything you earned or gained. Salary, rent, wages, electricity, advertising, discount allowed, loss by fire — and on the other side, sales, commission received, interest received, discount received. They're "nominal" — in name only — because at year end they are emptied into the Profit & Loss account and start again at zero.

Rule for nominal accounts
DEBIT
all expenses and losses

Money leaving to run the business goes on the left.

CREDIT
all incomes and gains

Money the business earns goes on the right.

Feel it: you pay ₹12,000 rent in cash. Rent is an expense — Dr Rent A/c. Cash is a real account going out — Cr Cash A/c. Notice how the two rules co-operate: one account is judged by its own family's rule, the other by its own.

The one thing to internalise

You never apply one rule to a whole transaction. You look at each account separately, ask what family it belongs to, and apply that family's rule to it. A single journal entry routinely uses two different rules.

Your turn

Practise on your own

Work each one out before you open it. Name the two accounts, name their types, then decide the sides.

Paid ₹3,200 for electricity in cash.
  • Dr Electricity A/c ₹3,200 — Nominal. An expense, and all expenses are debited.
  • Cr Cash A/c ₹3,200 — Real. Cash went out.
Rahul settles his ₹10,000 bill by cheque.
  • Dr Bank A/c ₹10,000 — Personal (artificial). The bank received the money on your behalf.
  • Cr Rahul A/c ₹10,000 — Personal (natural). Rahul gave it, so he is the giver.

Two personal accounts, one rule, applied twice.

Bought a delivery van worth ₹4,00,000 from Tata Motors on credit.
  • Dr Van A/c ₹4,00,000 — Real. The van came in.
  • Cr Tata Motors A/c ₹4,00,000 — Personal (artificial). They gave the van and are still owed.

Note what did not happen: no cash moved, and the entry still balances. Double entry tracks obligations, not just money.

Paid next year's insurance of ₹6,000 in advance, by cheque.
  • Dr Prepaid Insurance A/c ₹6,000 — Personal (representative). It stands for the insurer, who now owes you cover. It is not an expense yet, because the year it covers hasn't happened.
  • Cr Bank A/c ₹6,000 — Personal (artificial). The bank gave it.
Goods costing ₹9,000 were destroyed in a fire. No insurance.
  • Dr Loss by Fire A/c ₹9,000 — Nominal. A loss, and all losses are debited.
  • Cr Purchases A/c (or Stock) ₹9,000 — The goods went out of the business, just not through a sale.
Deposited ₹25,000 of the shop's cash into the business bank account.
  • Dr Bank A/c ₹25,000 — Personal (artificial). The bank received it.
  • Cr Cash A/c ₹25,000 — Real. Cash went out of the till.

Your wealth didn't change at all — this is why "debit = bad" makes no sense as a mental model.

Where people slip

Six traps that catch everyone

1. Cash is real, but Bank is personal

Cash in the drawer is a thing you possess — real. Money "in the bank" is not in your pocket; it is a debt the bank owes you, and a bank is an artificial person — personal. The two behave identically in every entry, which is exactly why the distinction only ever bites you in an exam.

2. Capital and Drawings are personal, not something exotic

The business and its owner are treated as two separate people. Money the owner puts in is the owner giving — credit Capital. Money the owner takes out is the owner receiving — debit Drawings. Once you accept that split, both accounts stop being mysterious.

3. "Outstanding" and "Prepaid" are people in disguise

Rent A/c is nominal. Outstanding Rent A/c is personal — it represents the landlord you still owe. Same for Prepaid Insurance, Accrued Commission, Salary Payable. If the word describes a timing difference rather than the expense itself, look for the person hiding behind it.

4. Your bank app uses the words backwards — on purpose

Your salary lands and the app says "credited". In your books, cash coming in is a debit. Both are right, because the app is showing you a page from the bank's ledger, not yours. To the bank you are a person they owe money to, so your balance sits on their credit side. Never calibrate your rules against a bank statement.

5. Purchases and Sales — textbooks genuinely disagree

Some books treat goods accounts (Purchases, Sales, Returns) as real, since goods are property coming in and going out. Others treat Purchases as an expense and Sales as an income, making both nominal. This is a real, long-standing split, not something you are misreading. Two things save you: the debit/credit answer is identical either way (Purchases Dr, Sales Cr), and you should simply follow whichever convention your own textbook or teacher uses.

6. One transaction, two different rules

The single most common beginner error is picking a rule for the transaction rather than for each account. "Paid rent in cash" is nominal on one side and real on the other. Always split first, classify second, then apply.

The other rulebook

What to do when someone says "assets and liabilities" instead

There is a second, newer classification — the modern or American approach. It is not a rival system; it is the same double entry sorted into different boxes.

The modern approach skips person/thing/why and sorts every account into five buckets: assets, liabilities, capital, income, expenses. Then it gives you one rule per bucket, based on whether the account is going up or down.

The mnemonic you'll see everywhere

DEAD CLIC. Things that increase on the debit side: Drawings, Expenses, Assets, Dividends. Things that increase on the credit side: Capital, Liabilities, Income, Credit sales revenue.

Run any transaction through both rulebooks and you get the same journal entry every time. If you are studying in India, the traditional three-way classification is what you'll be examined on; the modern one is what accounting software and most international texts assume. Knowing that they agree is what stops the two from ever confusing you.

Keep this · the three golden rules

  1. Personal — a who Debit the receiver, credit the giver.
  2. Real — a what Debit what comes in, credit what goes out.
  3. Nominal — a why Debit all expenses and losses, credit all incomes and gains.
The four-step method

1. Name the two (or more) accounts the transaction touches. 2. Classify each one through the flowchart. 3. Apply that account's own golden rule to decide its side. 4. Check that the left total equals the right total. If step 4 fails, step 1 was wrong — you missed an account.
