OHM does not change the price of polyol. It changes how many weeks of cover the company holds against what the index is doing, and how fast a price move turns into a buying or pricing decision. That is the difference between a 30% cost shock landing in one quarter and being spread, priced and partly avoided.

What the tagline does not claim

OHM does not touch the three largest costs: store leases (~₹95 cr), advertising (~₹115 cr) and delivery (~₹100 cr). It does not fix attrition, contract-labour mix or store site selection. Those stay with management. The claim is confined to stock, procurement and material-price timing.

1A. Inside the books: what is moving the bottom line

In plain words

Wakefit's profit looks healthy because of bank interest and an accounting entry. Underneath, the business of making and selling mattresses is being squeezed by shop rent, delivery, stock that does not sell, workers who leave, and shops that fail. Most of those are operations problems, not finance problems.

Seven things quietly taking money out, largest first.

  1. Shop rent. 165 rented shops, adding 80 a year. About ₹95 crore a year and set to double. Accounting rules put rent below the "operating profit" line, so the 7.5% operating margin is before rent.
  2. Advertising. About ₹115 crore a year; competitors are forcing it up.
  3. Delivery. About ₹100 crore a year. Roughly 8 rupees of every 100 earned goes on getting bulky products to the door. A mattress returned within 100 days goes back to the factory for rework.
  4. Contract workers. About ₹85 crore a year. 84 of every 100 people on the factory floor are contract workers. Of own staff, 35 to 65 in every 100 leave each year.
  5. Foam chemicals. The main mattress input rose 30 to 160% in March 2026; management expects mattress cost up about 30% once old stock is used. Prices are being raised to cover it.
  6. Stock that is not selling. ₹193 crore of stock, of which ₹27 crore is admitted to be slow or dead. Two years ago a quarter of all stock was in that state. Causes: 4,000 furniture designs with 3,000 new in a year, seven warehouses plus shops, and buying chemicals ahead of price rises.
  7. Shops that fail. For every 100 shops opened, 12 to 19 close within a year or two for "low revenue". Each cost ₹45 lakh to fit out plus rent while it failed.

Why the profit number flatters them. After the IPO there is ₹959 crore in the bank earning ₹60 to 70 crore a year; without that interest the shops and factories lost about ₹66 crore in FY25. And ₹98 crore of the FY26 profit of ₹189 crore is a one-time tax entry, not cash. Real FY26 profit was about ₹91 crore.

What the auditors found. For three years the stock figure given to the bank did not match the stock figure in the accounts. There was no internal audit team until listing preparation. The software kept no record of who changed what. The one asset that is both the bank's security and the biggest operational problem could not be counted reliably.

Why it matters for S-PAL. The levers that matter are operational: counting and controlling stock, choosing which shops to open and close, and managing delivery and workers. The finance items only change how the numbers look.

1A.2 Five things the headline numbers hide

  1. The store estate is the real fixed cost, and it sits below EBITDA. Lease liabilities grew from ₹103 cr (FY22) to ₹265 cr (Dec-2024). Under Ind AS 116 the rent appears as ₹37 cr of ROU depreciation plus ₹18 cr of lease interest in nine months, roughly ₹73 cr a year, heading toward ₹95 cr in FY26 and doubling again if 80 stores a year continue. Operating EBITDA of 7.5% is reported before all of it. The IPO fresh issue earmarked ₹145 cr just to pay leases on stores that already exist. [O: real estate decisions; F: presentation]
  1. Other income is carrying PBT. FY25 loss before tax was ₹35 cr after ₹31 cr of other income. The operating business lost about ₹66 cr. With ₹959 cr of investable cash after the IPO, treasury income will run at ₹60–70 cr a year and will make PBT look better than the stores and plants are. Separate the two when reading any quarter. [F]
  1. The FY26 profit of ₹189 cr includes a ₹98 cr deferred tax asset. At Dec-2024 the company had ₹100.6 cr of unrecognised deferred tax assets, mostly on ₹91 cr of carried-forward losses, and the auditors wrote that there was "no reasonable certainty" of enough future taxable profit to use them. Sixteen months later the company recognised ₹98 cr of it in a single quarter. It is non-cash, it is legitimate if the forecasts hold, and it reverses if they do not. Underlying FY26 PAT is about ₹91 cr. [F]
  1. Inventory has a provision problem that was never discussed. The management provision for slow-moving and non-moving stock was ₹1.9 cr at Mar-2022, ₹38.2 cr at Mar-2023, ₹28.5 cr at Mar-2024 and ₹27.5 cr at Dec-2024, against gross inventory of ₹193 cr, so 14% of stock is provided against today and it was about 25% in FY23. The FY23 charge of roughly ₹36 cr went through cost of materials and changes in inventory and is a large part of why FY23 gross margin was 42.7%. Raw material inventory then doubled from ₹61 cr to ₹105 cr in nine months to Dec-2024 as chemicals were pre-bought, which is why nine-month operating cash flow was only ₹6.5 cr on a ₹62 cr inventory build. [O: planning and SKU control; F: provisioning]
  1. Marketplace commission has not fallen in rupees. ₹49 cr in FY23, ₹49 cr in FY24, ₹48 cr in 9M FY25. The owned-channel story is true as a percentage but the external channel is still paying the same cheque. [F/O]

1A.6 Read-through for S-PAL

Six of the top eight items are operational, not financial: leases are a site-selection decision, logistics and contract labour are plant and warehouse decisions, inventory provisioning is a planning decision, closures are an estate decision, and raw-material pre-buying is a procurement decision. The finance items (other income, deferred tax, ESOP, gateway fees) change how the numbers look; the operational items change what the numbers are. The auditor findings tie the two together: for three years the one asset that carried both a bank charge and a double-digit provision, inventory, had two different numbers in the books and the bank filing.

3. Fallback rule applied: where sector assumptions substitute for company evidence

This account has unusually complete public data, so few levers are sector-assumed. The gaps that matter:

  1. Return and replacement rate on mattresses and furniture, and the reverse-logistics cost per return. The DRHP gives warranty provisions but not return volumes. [S]
  2. Store-level unit economics: revenue per store, payback, rent-to-sales. Management states breakeven targets for Jumbo (18–24 months) but not for regular stores. [S]
  3. Chemical purchase terms: spot vs contract, hedging, inventory cover in weeks. Management's "inventory benefit" comments imply pre-buying but not a policy. [S]
  4. FY26 line-item costs (courier, advertising, contract labour) at full-year level; only 9M FY25 and Q4 FY26 marketing are public. [S]
  5. Scrap and yield by plant; scrap sales value is known, yield loss is not. [S]
  6. Quick-commerce economics of the Bengaluru mattress pilot. [S]

Company facts to confirm early in discovery:

  • How much of Q1 FY27's 57.1% gross margin was old-inventory benefit vs price realisation.
  • Inventory composition at Sep-2025 (₹262 cr): raw chemicals pre-bought, finished goods for new stores, or slow-moving furniture SKUs.
  • The 8 store closures: what was known at signing vs. at closure.
  • Spring-mattress line plan.

Objections to prepare for

  • "We have SAP." Answer: the auditors said the stock statement and the books disagreed for three years and the software had no audit trail. SAP exists; one stock number does not.
  • "We pre-buy on purpose." Answer: yes, and management told investors the margin is borrowed from old inventory. Cover should be a decision per week with the index in front of you, not a quarterly bet.
  • "₹24 crore is small on ₹1,500 crore." Answer: it is a quarter of real profit, and the share price is a third below the IPO because the market is grading margin.
