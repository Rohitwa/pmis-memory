5.1 Why metal-rate parity is the lever that matters at Auto Ignition

Starter motors and alternators are among the most metal-intensive parts a tractor OEM buys. Copper windings, electrical-steel laminations, steel shafts and housings, aluminium die-castings and (on the EV line) NdFeB magnets. Indian OEM supply agreements compensate suppliers for metal movements through a PV clause tied to a published index (MCX/LME copper, steel benchmarks), reset quarterly or half-yearly, on a standard metal weight per part agreed at nomination.

Leakage between the clause and the P&L comes from four places, all of which require lot-level evidence to close:

#Leakage mechanismWhat goes wrong without taggingWhat OHM changes
L1Lag asymmetryOEM resets on the index with a one to two quarter lag. Supplier holds 50–60 days of stock. On a rising index the supplier is under-compensated for the quarter; on a falling index OEMs apply the cut immediately while the supplier consumes high-cost stock. CRISIL's sector view: pass-through "with a lag of one to two quarters and not always in full".Each lot carries its landed rate. Consumption in the period is valued at actual lot rate, giving a defensible claim for the gap and a factual basis to negotiate the reset frequency.
L2Weight and yield basisPV is paid on standard weight. Actual consumption includes lamination stamping yield loss, winding scrap, machining allowances. Typical gap 3–8% of standard weight. Without lot-level consumption data the supplier cannot substantiate the true metal content and absorbs the difference.RM-to-FG tagging produces actual consumption per part number, which supports revision of standard weights at the next nomination and PV reconciliation in the interim.
L3Scrap realisation5–10% of metal input becomes process scrap. Scrap is sold by weight at plant level, often below benchmark, and rarely reconciled against input.Scrap is tagged to source lot and metal grade; sale is reconciled to input and benchmarked to index.
L4Uncompensated Tier-2 metalBought-out sub-assemblies (bearings, drives, solenoids, magnets) carry metal that Tier-2s re-price upward, but the OEM PV clause does not cover them.Metal content of bought-outs is tagged, so the supplier can claim them under the PV clause or push back on Tier-2 pricing with evidence.

"Parity with OEMs" is therefore not a pricing negotiation. It is the ability to prove, transaction by transaction, that the metal the OEM compensated for equals the metal Auto Ignition actually paid for and consumed.

5.2 What the 35-point check needs to catch at this account

The 35 points have not been shared, so this is the target list of outflow leakages at an auto-electrical Tier-1 of this size, to be mapped against the actual checks:

  • Three-way match failures (PO, GRN, invoice) and rate deviations against PO or rate contract.
  • Duplicate or near-duplicate vendor invoices across four plants.
  • Payments released before due date, or without using agreed credit period.
  • Advances to vendors and tooling suppliers not adjusted against subsequent invoices.
  • Missed cash discounts and missed vendor debit notes (rejections, short supply, freight recovery).
  • Freight and logistics overbilling across four plants and export shipments.
  • Contract-labour billing against attendance (headcount spread of 389 to 1,000 suggests heavy contract use).
  • Capex invoice release against milestone completion at Supa and Satara.
  • GST input-credit mismatches that delay or lose credit.
  • Statutory and bank-charge outflows.

6.6 Objections to prepare for

  • "Our OEMs already compensate us on metal." Answer: on standard weight, with a lag, and not on bought-outs. Ask for the last four PV reconciliations.
  • "We have an ERP with lot control." Answer: lot numbers exist; lot rate carried through to consumption valuation and PV claim usually does not. Ask how the last claim was computed.

1A. Inside the books: what is moving the bottom line

In plain words

Wakefit's profit looks healthy because of bank interest and an accounting entry. Underneath, the business of making and selling mattresses is being squeezed by shop rent, delivery, stock that does not sell, workers who leave, and shops that fail. Most of those are operations problems, not finance problems.

Seven things quietly taking money out, largest first.

  1. Shop rent. 165 rented shops, adding 80 a year. About ₹95 crore a year and set to double. Accounting rules put rent below the "operating profit" line, so the 7.5% operating margin is before rent.
  2. Advertising. About ₹115 crore a year; competitors are forcing it up.
  3. Delivery. About ₹100 crore a year. Roughly 8 rupees of every 100 earned goes on getting bulky products to the door. A mattress returned within 100 days goes back to the factory for rework.
  4. Contract workers. About ₹85 crore a year. 84 of every 100 people on the factory floor are contract workers. Of own staff, 35 to 65 in every 100 leave each year.
  5. Foam chemicals. The main mattress input rose 30 to 160% in March 2026; management expects mattress cost up about 30% once old stock is used. Prices are being raised to cover it.
  6. Stock that is not selling. ₹193 crore of stock, of which ₹27 crore is admitted to be slow or dead. Two years ago a quarter of all stock was in that state. Causes: 4,000 furniture designs with 3,000 new in a year, seven warehouses plus shops, and buying chemicals ahead of price rises.
  7. Shops that fail. For every 100 shops opened, 12 to 19 close within a year or two for "low revenue". Each cost ₹45 lakh to fit out plus rent while it failed.

Why the profit number flatters them. After the IPO there is ₹959 crore in the bank earning ₹60 to 70 crore a year; without that interest the shops and factories lost about ₹66 crore in FY25. And ₹98 crore of the FY26 profit of ₹189 crore is a one-time tax entry, not cash. Real FY26 profit was about ₹91 crore.

What the auditors found. For three years the stock figure given to the bank did not match the stock figure in the accounts. There was no internal audit team until listing preparation. The software kept no record of who changed what. The one asset that is both the bank's security and the biggest operational problem could not be counted reliably.

Why it matters for S-PAL. The levers that matter are operational: counting and controlling stock, choosing which shops to open and close, and managing delivery and workers. The finance items only change how the numbers look.

Read-through

The group's profit is high and its costs are falling, and both are true. Underneath, three structural things decide what the owner keeps: commodity prices he does not set, mine and permit timing he does not fully control, and a holding-company debt that takes $1.1 billion a year out of the operating companies before anyone else. The operational findings cluster in two places: outsourced O&M at power plants, where 24 people died, and procurement, where the group runs on 64 days of supplier credit across thousands of vendors and more than a dozen sites. Those two are where a controls conversation starts.
